Measuring and Improving Transfer of Neural Surrogates for Vehicle Aerodynamics

Favour Nerrise, Julian Busch, Gurpreet Singh Hora, Martin Sipka, Danielle C. Maddix, Georgia Olympia Brikis

Neural surrogates for external vehicle aerodynamics are fast alternatives to physics-based computational fluid dynamics (CFD) simulations. Their foundational limitation is a lack of zero-shot generalization across vehicle classes, which results in an expensive, custom training campaign for each new vehicle design. Similar to pretrained models in other fields, a pretrained surrogate should adapt to a new vehicle class using only a few simulations and stay accurate on its pretraining domain. No prior study has systematically tested this transfer behavior. We conduct a large-scale, controlled benchmarking study on five DrivAerML-pretrained surrogates across four independent target datasets. These datasets differ from the source in geometry, CFD solver, turbulence model, and flow conditions. We find that fine-tuning these surrogates outperforms training from scratch in fewer than half of the comparisons and lowers the accuracy of the surrogate on its source dataset. We introduce GeoReplay, a geometry- and gradient-aligned source replay that keeps source simulations resembling the target in training and applies their updates only when they agree with the target gradient. A risk bound separates geometric coverage from differences in the simulated physics, and we prove that every sufficiently small GeoReplay step decreases the target training objective. GeoReplay lowers the source error of fine-tuning in 77 of 80 settings and matches its target accuracy.

Singular layer neural networks for the Navier-Stokes equations at small viscosity

Teng-Yuan Chang, Gung-Min Gie, Youngjoon Hong, Chang-Yeol Jung

We develop neural network methods for the two-dimensional incompressible Navier-Stokes equations at a small viscosity $\varepsilon > 0$, whose accuracy is uniform in $\varepsilon$ and whose residual is evaluated on a grid that does not resolve the vorticity boundary layer. In the vorticity-stream function formulation with the slip boundary conditions, a vorticity layer of width $\sqrt{\varepsilon}$ forms along the walls. The trial function of the singular layer Physics-Informed Neural Network (sl-PINN) contains the corrector of the asymptotic analysis, together with the law that fixes its amplitude at the wall. The amplitude is tied to the wall trace of the network itself, so that the method requires no a priori knowledge of the limit solution. The sl-PINN keeps the error uniform in $\varepsilon$ over six decades, for the Stokes and the Navier-Stokes equations alike, although in the latter the wall trace travels with the flow and is not known in advance. An enrichment that uses the thin scale but leaves the profile and the amplitude of the layer free deteriorates as $\varepsilon\to0$. Since the corrector carries the layer analytically, the Laplacian in the residual can be discretized by a finite volume flux difference on a coarse grid. The resulting singular layer Finite Volume Neural Network (sl-FVNN) attains the same uniform accuracy as the sl-PINN, to within the scatter of the random seed, at about half the cost of a training step. The combination of the asymptotic corrector with the coarse-grid finite volume discretization is the main result of this article.

Stochastic Representation-Inspired Physics-Informed Neural Networks for Incompressible Fluid Flows

Zihao Guo, Zhongmin Qian

The effectiveness of neural networks in solving partial differential equations depends not only on their approximation capability but also on the suitability of their representations for the underlying solutions. Motivated by stochastic integral representations of incompressible fluid flows and the associated Biot--Savart kernel, we propose a Biot--Savart-informed (i.e. physics-informed) neural network (BS-PINN) for the incompressible Navier--Stokes equations. By extracting a rational profile from a regularized Biot--Savart kernel, we construct a smooth activation function with fixed and learnable kernel scales. Both variants retain the conventional velocity--pressure formulation and physics-informed training framework, allowing the influence of the activation function to be examined independently. We evaluate the method on five benchmarks, including Kovasznay flow, cylinder wake, three-dimensional Beltrami flow, Taylor--Green vortex, and double shear layer. Across 21 benchmark--architecture comparisons, both variants consistently reduce the combined velocity error relative to the conventional $\tanh$-based PINN, achieving mean relative error reductions of 31.7\% and 33.2\%, respectively. Improvements are also observed in reported pressure and vorticity errors. Notably, the double-shear-layer experiments demonstrate improved resolution of localized shear structures and, for BS-PINN-F, qualitatively clearer vortex-roll-up patterns in the long-time simulation. These findings highlight the potential of incorporating analytical flow structures into neural-network representations for incompressible flow simulations.

AeroZoo: A Benchmark of Well-Posed Flow Problems for Foundation Models of Aerodynamics

Peter Sharpe, Carmelo Gonzales, Corey Adams, Mehdi Ataei, Sanjay Choudhry

Neural surrogates for computational fluid dynamics (CFD) are trained and ranked on datasets that each vary one design family in one flow domain. Such datasets cannot test the least that an aerodynamic foundation model must do: predict CFD-quality incompressible flow in seconds for new shapes in new enclosures. On DrivAerML, larger models and longer training barely lower Transolver's error; doubling the training cases cuts it by about 12% up to 400 cases, exhausting the dataset. We introduce AeroZoo, a benchmark and training corpus of 3,815 time-mean solutions to the unsteady Reynolds-averaged Navier--Stokes (RANS) equations with a transition model, across seven geometry families, eight enclosure configurations and Reynolds numbers from $10^3$ to $10^8$. Each sample poses a well-posed PDE problem in the form a solver receives it: the boundary meshes grouped by condition type, the prescribed values and the fluid properties. The 2,816 samples with a stable time mean form the canonical benchmark: two tasks, engineering metrics and 22 frozen splits, disjoint in geometry, that hold out geometry families, enclosures, boundary-condition layouts and Reynolds ranges. On the random split, boundary-condition tokens cut GeoTransolver's median velocity error from 76.9% to 8.4% of the inlet speed in single-seed runs (uniform inlet flow: 11.0%). ISLA, a transformer for boundary-driven PDEs, reaches 5.3% over four seeds, about 4 times the change from a finer mesh. On the same test samples, a held-out geometry family barely changes ISLA's error relative to uniform flow, and a held-out enclosure or boundary-condition layout raises it 2.4 to 9.2 times. The data and the scoring code will be released.

$H_2$-optimal riblet profiles for turbulence suppression in channel flows

Daniel W. Hartman, Armin Zare

The drag-reducing properties of streamwise-elongated surface corrugations, commonly referred to as riblets, have been extensively studied through experiments, simulations, and reduced-order modeling. Notably, the linearized Navier-Stokes framework introduced by Ran, Zare, and Jovanovic (J. Fluid Mech., vol. 906, 2021) employs volume penalization to model riblet-induced boundary conditions and predict drag reduction. These approaches typically quantify drag reduction as a function of a single geometric parameter, such as spanwise spacing or groove cross-sectional area, evaluated over fixed riblet shapes. As a result, identifying optimal riblet geometries across a broader design space often necessitates exhaustive parametric studies. To address this problem, we propose an optimization framework for the systematic design of optimal riblet shapes. Accordingly, we reformulate the volume penalization term in the linearized dynamics as a structured state-feedback operator. Targeting maximal suppression of turbulence, we pose a composite optimization problem that minimizes the steady-state variance of velocity fluctuations and riblet-induced dynamical augmentations while maintaining a constant bulk-flux. For a fixed spanwise wavelength (riblet spacing), this framework yields optimal wall-normal profiles that achieve maximal turbulence attenuation.

A curvature-corrected port-Hamiltonian reduced-order model for dissipative sloshing with contact-line hysteresis in low-gravity tanks

Gianni Cassoni, Fabrizio Gennari, Jacopo Serafini, Pierangelo Masarati

A physics-based reduced-order model unifies curved-meniscus inertia, viscous losses and nonsmooth contact-line stick-slip in a passive port-Hamiltonian formulation for lateral sloshing in cylindrical tanks. Dirichlet-Neumann and Jacobi operators describe inertia and restoring forces, while a convex potential combines bulk, Stokes-layer and contact-line dissipation. Analytical propagation between stick-slip events supports faster-than-real-time simulation of coupled spacecraft translation and sloshing. The viscous closure agrees within 5% with a reference Stokes calculation, and the model reproduces the Henderson-Miles modal measurements and the Cocciaro hysteretic decay. Across 42 drop-tower and ground measurements, curvature correction reduces the root-mean-square frequency error from 24 to 4%. For flight and ground tests from the Middeck 0-Gravity Dynamics Experiment (MODE), frequency errors range from 0.9 to 7.1%, while ideal contact lines underestimate the damping ratios from the MODE fits by 38-87%. One hysteresis width, calibrated on the lowest orbital water excitation, predicts higher orbital excitations and the ground test with normalised force errors of 11-35%, against 149-208% for an ideal pinned line. It predicts 8.5% resonance softening against 7.3% measured, which the tested one-parameter damping laws miss. Uncertain wetting and remaining response discrepancies motivate damping ranges in spacecraft control analyses.

Direct Numerical Simulation of Vortex-Induced Instability Over a Semi-Infinite Flat Plate with Discrete Roughness

Aditi Sengupta, Tapan K. Sengupta, Vajjala K. Suman

The receptivity of flow over a semi-infinite flat plate to a combination of deterministic free stream excitation and surface roughness, is investigated by solving the incompressible Navier-Stokes equation (NSE). The free stream excitation is in the form of a convecting vortex with fixed strength and sign, while the roughness element is a Gaussian bump. Two-dimensional (2D) and three-dimensional (3D) direct numerical simulations (DNS) are performed to determine whether the nonmodal nonlinear nature of transition, previously established for the vortex-induced instability in [Sundaram et al., Effects of free stream excitation on the boundary layer over a semi-infinite flat plate, Phys. Fluids, 32, 094110 (2020))], holds in the presence of surface roughness. The study aims to capture the fundamental differences arising due to the addition of surface roughness using instantaneous flow features, vorticity dynamics and application of nonlinear theories of receptivity. Roughness accelerates the transition process significantly, while retaining the indicator of nonmodal response, i.e. the spatio-temporal wave front (STWF). The inherently 2D nature of the vortex-induced instability evolves to three-dimensionality much faster in the presence of roughness, necessitating a 3D framework to capture the effects of roughness accurately. The cumulative response to the surface bump and convecting vortex can be separated into four stages, viz. are (i) generation of vortical disturbances at the roughness, (ii) a spatio-temporal amplification of the disturbance field, (iii) emergence and amplification of the STWF marked by sequence of unsteady separation bubbles, and (iv) emergence of turbulent flow due to merging of the unsteady separation bubbles to turbulent spots.

Kinetic study of Reaction-Control-Jet Interference in Rarefied Hypersonic Crossflow

Alexis Theofilis, Martin Grabe

A 1975 wind-tunnel study at the German Aerospace Center (DLR) of a helium jet blowing from a flat plate into a Mach 11 to 12 air crossflow at three freestream densities is revisited using two- and three-dimensional Direct Simulation Monte Carlo (DSMC) computations at Knudsen numbers 0.021 to 0.125. The planar slot used in two-dimensional model computations reproduces the shock topology, the sign and two-lobed structure of the interference pressure and its trend with rarefaction, and its plate-alone boundary layer is consistent with the three-dimensional one and with a similarity solution with slip and temperature jump. However, two-dimensional simulations overpredict the peak interference pressure about twelvefold at every condition and carry it farther upstream. A resolution sweep at the most rarefied condition converges the planar interference to within 1% of its extrapolated limit. Three-dimensional DSMC reproduces the measured interference pressure at all three densities, within 25% at the lobe peak and 3 mm in its position, although two- and three-dimensional simulations overpredict the plate-alone wall pressure by 50 to 75%. Continuum breakdown around the jet persists even at the lowest Knudsen number examined. Fully three-dimensional kinetic simulations are underway to extend the present validation to a comparison of jet strength and forces over wider Knudsen number ranges, and to an examination of lateral relief as a reason for the discrepancy.

PhysDial: Adapting Pretrained Video Models for PDE Simulation under Changing Physical Conditions

Yilong Dai, Yiming Sun, Shengyu Chen, Jiaqi Gong, Xiaowei Jia, Runlong Yu

Simulating how physical systems respond to changing conditions is central to control, design, and scenario studies. To our knowledge, prior PDE foundation models have not been trained or evaluated on changes within trajectories. Our counterfactual test measures how far predictions move toward a changed solver branch from the same state. Models trained on fixed conditions follow changes only partly. A PDE foundation model given a condition input after pretraining barely responds. PhysDial adapts a pretrained video diffusion transformer to eight two-dimensional PDE families. Coefficients enter with the noise level, condition fields as reference frames, and equation text as a prompt. In Kolmogorov flow, switches within trajectories account for about 5% of the training samples. At matched compute, they halve the error after a change in forcing wavenumber or amplitude. A few trajectories at new amplitudes more than halve extrapolation error. Without physics pretraining, PhysDial matches or exceeds PDE foundation models under training conditions on two turbulent flows. At step 64 on the Kolmogorov test split, PhysDial has 1.07 times the solver's small-scale energy and Walrus 7.1 times. Our experiments suggest that a learned simulator uses a condition only as far as its training data make that condition necessary for prediction. Learning from trajectories that diverge under intervention can help scientific simulators predict what would happen if a system's forcing or parameters changed.

A boundary integral formulation for microscale Marangoni surfing

Scott Weady

We consider a model for collections of surfactant-producing particles moving along the surface of a viscous fluid under their self-generated Marangoni flows. Such systems are challenging due to their nonlocal nature: particle motion is coupled to Marangoni stresses distributed across the entire fluid surface and to the resulting flows in the bulk. In the low Reynolds and Péclet number limit, we reduce these nonlocal effects to a system of integral equations supported solely on the particles and their boundaries. This formulation is not only computationally tractable, but also provides a framework for systematically coarse-graining many-particle systems. Numerical simulations, performed with a custom spectral method, show collective dynamics are highly sensitive to shape. In particular, among elliptical particles, we find prolate particles tend to attract while oblate particles tend to repel, and these geometric details control the stability of polarized populations. We rationalize these behaviors through a far-field expansion of the boundary integral equations and its corresponding reduced-order model.

By lifting features into a higher-dimensional manifold, this operator significantly enhances the model's representation capacity. Experiments demonstrate that this approach enables strong reconstruction performance and zero-shot generalization, offering a physics-aware inductive bias for super-resolution of three-dimensional turbulence. More broadly, this work suggests that high-dimensional operators offer a promising avenue for modeling intricate multi-scale turbulent flows.

Physics-based Tabulation Approach for Rotating Detonation Engines

Noah Schwalb, Vansh Sharma, Shivank Sharma, Venkat Raman

High-fidelity simulations of rotating detonation engines remain computationally expensive due to the strong coupling between compressible flow dynamics and stiff chemical kinetics. The coexistence of deflagrative and detonative combustion, along with shock interactions and rapid thermodynamic changes, further challenges conventional reduced-order chemistry models. This work presents a multi-mode tabulation framework that replaces detailed chemical kinetic integration with precomputed thermochemical states and source terms while accounting for variations in the local thermodynamic state. The key novelty lies in a unified treatment of distinct combustion regimes, wherein homogeneous reactor solutions describe deflagrative combustion and post-detonation reactions up to the Chapman-Jouguet (CJ) point, while an equilibrium isentropic expansion model captures supersonic relaxation beyond the CJ state. The framework is evaluated against detailed chemistry across progressively complex configurations, including homogeneous ignition, one-dimensional detonations, and a two-dimensional rotating detonation engine (RDE). The tabulated model accurately reproduces ignition delays and captures the initiation and sustained propagation of detonation waves across varying mixture compositions. Furthermore, the framework reconstructs the complete thermodynamic evolution along an RDE streamline, including post-injection expansion, detonation, post-detonation expansion, and oblique shock interactions, directly from conserved flow variables. These results demonstrate the capability of multi-mode tabulation to represent coupled combustion, shock, and expansion processes within a single thermodynamically responsive framework, offering a promising route toward computationally efficient simulations of detonative combustion across a broad range of applications.

Where hurricanes get their spin: angular momentum versus kinetic energy

Boris Galperin, Jun A. Zhang

Tropical cyclones almost never form within a few degrees of the equator. Their energy source, the heat engine fueled by the warm ocean, is available there as everywhere else. What is missing must be the other ingredient a cyclone needs, absolute angular momentum about the local vertical. Across reconnaissance intensity categories, characteristic wind energy increases and the radius of maximum wind decreases, while the characteristic specific absolute angular momentum $M$ varies comparatively little; energy and absolute angular momentum are thus supplied separately. We propose that $M$ is drawn from the mesoscale turbulence in which storms form, and measure the rotational content of this turbulence in ERA5 winds over the western Pacific. Including the storms, the rotational part of the one-dimensional spectrum along latitude circles follows the Coriolis branch $Cf^2k^{-3}$ of rotating stratified turbulence, with $C=0.047$, about a third of the theoretical value, from $12^\circ$ to $24^\circ$N. Between storms, this value is lower and grows, approximately, as $f$; we interpret the storms as its magnified, predominantly cyclonic tail. Toward the equator the energy of the turbulence persists but its rotational content collapses: equatorward of $\sim6^\circ$N, more than one third of the energy is divergent. Typhoon Vamei, which formed at $1.5^\circ$N in December 2001, was preceded by rotational variance above the 99th percentile of December climatology.

Stabilized negative viscosity governs ocean jets before, during and after they leave the coast

Boris Galperin, Alexei Chekhlov, Ilya Staroselsky, Semion Sukoriansky

Eddies in rotating turbulence can feed the jets that spawn them, resulting in the negative viscosity phenomenon. Taken alone, a negative viscosity represents up-gradient eddy fluxes and is often referred to as "antifriction" or "antidiffusion". Mathematically, it leads to an ill-posed problem. In two-dimensional turbulence theory, this problem can be resolved by representing the transfer from small to large scales by a stabilized negative viscosity (SNV), in which a negative Laplacian viscosity is balanced by its positive biharmonic counterpart. In purely two-dimensional turbulence, by the Taylor identity, a negative viscosity is up-gradient transport of vorticity, which mixing cannot produce; on a slope, mixing of potential vorticity can mimic it, and we test for this. Using two years of wide-swath satellite altimetry (SWOT), we measure the eddy momentum flux across the five subtropical western boundary currents in each current's own frame. Along the continental slope the eddies act with a negative viscosity of order $-10^{3}$ m$^{2}$ s$^{-1}$, with the SNV structure resolved in the Gulf Stream and the Agulhas. At separation from the slope the sign reverses in all five currents, and the eddies brake the flow. Downstream, in the free jets, the negative viscosity returns on the cyclonic flank, strengthened to $-10^{4}$ m$^{2}$ s$^{-1}$. Separation is where the current leaves the strong topographic $β$ of the slope, the constraint that orders the eddy transfer. In an eddy-resolving reanalysis the SNV form holds across three ocean basins, with its shape parameter set by the deformation radius.

To examine the phase dependence of the wake and lift responses, the same vortex gust is imposed at $64$ shedding phases. For these phase-controlled responses, proper orthogonal decomposition (POD) with time-independent spatial modes characterizes departures from the periodic wake, whereas tDMDpc resolves evolving structures and the frequency and growth rate of the fundamental mode. In this decomposition, the approaching gust is mainly captured by the zero-frequency mode. During the encounter, however, gust-related structures develop in the fundamental mode and persist near the cylinder as the gust convects downstream. The fundamental-mode coefficient magnitude is nearly uniform across phases at arrival but becomes phase dependent afterward. Consistent with this phase dependence, the near-wake vorticity amplitude of the fundamental conjugate pair decreases at $α=π/2$ and increases at $α=3π/2$ relative to the respective pre-encounter values, matching the inward and outward departures of the POD trajectories. To connect these structures to the cylinder lift, modal pressure and viscous stresses are integrated over the cylinder surface. The zero-frequency contribution carries most of the lift variation at arrival, whereas the fundamental conjugate pair dominates later oscillations. Phase averaging cannot separate these contributions because the fundamental oscillations no longer cancel across phases after the encounter. Their separation reveals that opposing contributions can yield nearly zero total lift despite an appreciable fundamental contribution. As the wake recovers, the lift amplitude approaches its undisturbed value, but a phase shift persists.

Buoyancy-induced transitions in mixed convective flow past a heated square cylinder

Rudram Dushe, Arun K Saha

The effect of aiding buoyancy on flow past a square cylinder has previously been shown to suppress vortex shedding above a critical Richardson number (Ri), transitioning from unsteady to steady flow. Building on this, the present study uses two-dimensional direct numerical simulations (DNS) of vertically upward flow past a heated square cylinder to examine two buoyancy-induced transitions for a range of Ri and Reynolds number (Re). The secondary flow induced by buoyant force enhances positive entrainment in the momentum-deficit region aft of the cylinder, thereby suppressing vortex shedding. At higher Ri, this buoyant flow continues to gain downstream momentum, leading to a far-field plume-like instability. Extended domain simulations show that the far-field transition depends on the downstream distance available for buoyancy-driven disturbances to grow. Increasing Re delays near-field suppression but advances far-field instability. A local-flow analysis shows that a Reynolds number based on local mean velocity and plume width substantially improves the collapse of the far-field instability onset, while the corresponding plume-based Strouhal number also exhibits an approximate collapse across the investigated cases. Correlations for drag, surface-averaged Nusselt number, and far-field frequency are proposed using a mixed-convective velocity scale. Linear stability analysis (LSA) about the two-dimensional base flow captures both transitions as Hopf bifurcations and accurately predicts the dominant frequencies and finite-domain transition thresholds observed in DNS. The corresponding eigenmodes reveal localized oscillatory structures that shift downstream with increasing Ri. These results demonstrate the dual role of buoyancy in stabilizing the near wake while destabilizing the far field.

We present a framework for constructing deployable hard-constrained turbulence closures of unclosed terms in the transport equations of Reynolds stresses and turbulent dissipation rate. The framework is built from modules, each performing a single task. The closure module represents the closure with a small network, while the state module represents the flow state during training. The closure network is coupled to the state network through the governing equation residuals, allowing the closure to be learned without reference closure values being supplied as training data. The coordinate module learns monotone input coordinates that help both networks resolve sharp changes. Within the network architecture, the boundary condition (BC) module imposes boundary values exactly using the Theory of Functional Connections, and the realizability module keeps the closure realizable. We apply the framework to decaying homogeneous isotropic turbulence (HIT) with experimental and direct numerical simulation trajectories. Here, the state module is a Deep Operator Network (DeepONet), the BC module imposes the initial state and the closure's low and high Reynolds number limits, and the realizability module ensures growth of the Taylor microscale. Across ten random seeds, the framework reduces root-mean-square seed-to-seed closure variation by about 95\% relative to the unconstrained model over the training and held-out range. The deployed 18-parameter closure network requires only the Taylor scale Reynolds number and can be exported as a standalone C function.

For multilayer panels, the fluid-facing material determines the interfacial wave speed, while the subsurface stiffness distribution modifies the panel's effective thickness. Temporal stability analysis identifies regions of parameter space where a steady harmonic response is relevant and regions where instabilities grow. Together, these tools provide a basis for screening coating designs and selecting candidates for high-fidelity simulations and experiments.

Consistent interfacial flow numerics - Part II: The momentum convection term

Johan Roenby

The two-phase Volume-of-Fluid solvers of OpenFOAM overpredict the horizontal water velocity near the crest of a water wave, and the error grows as the wave propagates. Part I of this series removed the spurious air velocities caused by the gravity term but left this error unchanged. Here we show that it is caused by the momentum convection term. Its mass flux is computed in the interface advection step from the velocity at the start of the time step, whereas the convected velocity is taken at the end of the step, because convection is discretised implicitly. In a progressive wave, where the horizontal and vertical velocities are 90 degrees out of phase, this time lag produces an upward flux of horizontal momentum, proportional to the time step, which accumulates in a spurious current near the surface. The same mismatch makes the response to a force depend on the density at the wrong time level. Explicit convection removes the lag and makes the response consistent with the gravity force, but requires the convected face velocity at the interface to be taken from the upwind cell. Only then is the new velocity of a cell a weighted average of old velocities, whatever the density ratio. We demonstrate both requirements with prescribed flows and with the full solver. For a progressive wave, explicit convection with upwind face velocities at the interface reduces the overprediction of the crest velocity from 26-31 % to within 1 % and removes the near-surface current. The resulting solver, fluxinator, is meant for maximum Courant numbers of 0.1 and below, where it keeps the wave height within 1.5 % over 15 periods on meshes with six or more cells per wave height. Because the spurious velocities that limit the time step are removed, it needs 34-42 % less wall-clock time than interFoam for the tested wave.
