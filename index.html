TurtLES: A Large-Scale Benchmark for Turbulent 3D Neural PDE Surrogates

Jarno Platenburg, Armand Kassaï Koupaï, Paola Cinnella, Patrick Gallinari

Machine-learning surrogates are increasingly used to accelerate computational fluid dynamics, yet progress is limited by the lack of benchmarks capturing realistic, time-dependent turbulent flows. We introduce TurtLES, a 14 TB dataset of 3D turbulent cylinder wakes in the shear-layer transition regime, computed via high-fidelity implicit large-eddy simulations. The dataset contains 400 trajectories of 400 time steps each, with 3-11 million points per frame, spanning variations in geometry, Reynolds number, and angle of attack. Unlike existing datasets, TurtLES combines (i) physically realistic turbulence with an active energy cascade driven by vortex stretching, (ii) dense irregular meshes, and (iii) long-horizon temporal dynamics. We benchmark state-of-the-art neural operators on autoregressive rollouts and identify key failure modes, including temporal instability, loss of small-scale energy, and challenges in learning on large irregular domains. TurtLES provides a new benchmark for evaluating surrogate models on 3D turbulent wakes at industrially relevant mesh resolution.

A Conservative Darcy-Brinkman-Stokes Formulation for Digital Rock Flow Simulation with Embedded Second-Brinkman Effects

Gospel E. Stewart, Hannah P. Menke, David Egya, Jacqueline Mifsud, Julien Maes

Accurate pore-scale direct numerical simulation (DNS) typically requires boundary-conforming meshes on fully resolved geometries, which is memory-intensive and often becomes impractical when modelling complex rock geometries. In contrast, the Darcy-Brinkman-Stokes (DBS) framework enables simulations to be performed directly on voxelized micro-CT images. However, the commonly used simplified DBS formulation (DBS1) neglects the second Brinkman term (SBT) and the porosity-gradient viscous-diffusion term, leading to errors in flow-field characterisation and permeability prediction. Although it is well established that the SBT is essential for capturing viscous boundary effects, it is often assumed that its inclusion requires separate discretisation. In this work, we show that no separate discretisation of the SBT is necessary: writing the DBS equation in its conservative form and applying a standard central differencing scheme in a finite-volume discretisation naturally retains the full DBS formulation. We refer to this formulation as DBS2, and validate its implementation in the GeoChemFoam framework (our open-source computational fluid dynamics simulator based on OpenFOAM) using three benchmark cases: (i) microchannel flow, (ii) a 2D heterogeneous micromodel, and (iii) a 3D micro-CT image of Bentheimer sandstone. Simulation results show that the DBS2 formulation reproduces the DNS velocity field exactly in the microchannel and matches DNS permeability to within 1.5-3.8% in the micromodel and Bentheimer cases at matched spatial resolution. In contrast, DBS1 systematically overpredicts permeability (14.1 to 37.9% in our cases) under the same discretization. Notably, the conservative treatment of the viscous stress preserves the computational efficiency of DBS1 while achieving DNS-level accuracy.

Direct numerical simulation of turbulent heat transfer in water with temperature-dependent viscosity and conductivity

Davide Modesti, Alessandro Ceci

We study turbulent forced convection of liquid water in a plane channel by direct numerical simulation (DNS), with temperature-dependent viscosity and thermal conductivity. The simulations cover wall heating and cooling, mean-to-wall temperature ratios from 0.83 to 1.3, wall temperatures from 277 to 360\,K and wall Prandtl numbers from 2 to 12. Compared with constant-property flow at the same bulk Reynolds number, the friction coefficient changes by $-39\,\%$ to $+32\,\%$, the heat transfer coefficient referred to the wall Prandtl number changes by up to a factor of $2.3$, and empirical correlations deviate from the DNS by up to $26\,\%$. Instantaneous fields and spectra show that the wall friction Reynolds number misrepresents the range of turbulent scales, whereas near-wall streaks of velocity and temperature have a common spacing when scaled with the local viscous length. The density is constant, so the mean velocity and temperature defects in the core follow those of constant-property flow. To predict friction and heat transfer, we map the mean profiles onto those of constant-property flow through integral transformations derived from the mean momentum and energy balances, with empirical corrections of the eddy viscosity and diffusivity calibrated on the DNS. This approach, developed for air, is extended to liquids, whose conductivity is not proportional to the viscosity and whose Prandtl number varies across the channel. The inverse transformations predict friction and heat transfer within $2\,\%$ of the DNS, except at the lowest Reynolds numbers. The same corrections, introduced in the Spalart--Allmaras model, halve its friction error.

Modelling velocity gradient invariants in turbulence with Lundgren spiral vortex ensemble

Zhi Zheng, Weiyu Shen, Jin-Han Xie, Yue Yang

We investigate the joint probability density function $P(Q,R)$ of invariants $Q$ and $R$ of the velocity gradient tensor in the synthetic turbulent field formed from the Lundgren spiral vortex ensemble, which is referred to as the woven Lundgren turbulence (WLT). The WLT inherently captures non-Gaussian vorticity correlations and multiscale interactions, which are essential for reproducing $P(Q,R)$ observed in homogeneous isotropic turbulence. By analyzing the contributions of stretching, winding and dissipation processes in WLT, we demonstrate that the $Q$-$R$ diagram can be divided into three distinct physical regions: a head region dominated by stretching, a body region dominated by spiral vortex arms, and a tail region dominated by dissipation. We systematically vary three key parameters in WLT, the background strain rate, the cutoff age of spiral vortices and the viscous decay factor, and monitor the evolution of $P(Q,R)$ in different topological regions. It is revealed that increasing the stretching rate broadens the head region, larger cutoff ages enhance the inclination of the body region, and reducing viscous factors extends the tail region, thereby mimicking the effects of strain self-amplification and vortex stretching across scales. Based on WLT statistics, we establish and validate an explicit model of $P(Q,R)$ consisting of the stretching, spiral and dissipation contributions. Thus, the WLT provides a physically interpretable framework for understanding the relation between local geometry and global statistics of turbulence.

Numerical Investigations on Bitumen Liberation from Oil Sands Lump

Mohammadhossein Golchin, Siyu Chen, Payman Esmaeili, Petr Nikrityuk, Xuehua Zhang

The dynamic liberation and digestion of viscous oil-solid aggregates under turbulent flow is a critical transport phenomenon in multiphase chemical processes, including slurry transport, soil remediation, and industrial extraction. A primary industrial application of this phenomenon is oil sands hydrotransport, where pipelines are widely utilized to simultaneously transport and extract bitumen from lump ore. However, optimizing operating conditions requires a fundamental understanding of the localized hydrodynamic mechanisms governing detachment. To address this, a three-dimensional (3D) Eulerian-Eulerian (E-E) CFD approach coupled with k-omega SST turbulence model has been utilized to investigate bitumen detachment dynamics from complex solid matrices. Six numerical cases were analyzed across lump diameters (D) of 4 and 5 cm and particle Reynolds numbers (Re_p) between 2000 and 4000. The simulation results reveal that bitumen liberation is a shear-driven process, occurring only when the shear stresses exerted by the flow on the lump surface exceed a critical threshold value. The bitumen liberation rate increases with higher Re_p and smaller D, due to enhanced local shear forces and reduced particle shielding. Based on these mechanistic insights, a novel semi-empirical shear-driven model was formulated. The proposed model demonstrates excellent predictive accuracy when validated against experimental liberation data, with an error less than 8.3%. Overall, these findings clarify the fluid-particle interactions governing bitumen detachment and offer a robust, physics-based tool for predicting recovery efficiency in industrial hydrotransport operations.

Projection-based segregated, iterative, and multi-stage physics-informed neural network for incompressible Navier-stokes equations

Zhenyu Zhang, Zhixiang Ling, Yinghao Huo, Huayong Liu, Yunde Su

Physics-informed neural networks(PINNs) offer a mesh-free and continuously differentiable approach to fluid simulation. However, standard monolithic PINNs for incompressible Navier-Stokes (NS) equations often suffer from severe constraint coupling and spectral biases, limiting both convergence and accuracy. To address this challenge, we propose a segregated, iterative, and multi-stage PINN (SIM-PINN) inspired by the classical projection method in computational fluid dynamics. In SIM-PINN, the nonlinear NS equations are decoupled into the linearized momentum and pressure Poisson equations. Each is addressed separately using dedicated multi-stage neural networks and coupled through projection-based inner iterations. Each stage of the multi-stage neural networks for the momentum equations corresponds to one iteration of the projection method, in which the velocity fields are progressively refined at targeted frequencies. Upon iteration convergence, solutions to the fully coupled and nonlinear NS equations are recovered. By integrating the segregated and iterative framework with the multi-stage training, SIM-PINN alleviates the optimization difficulties of the incompressible NS equations arising from nonlinearity, constraint coupling, and spectral biases. The improved convergence and accuracy are demonstrated by simulations of steady lid-driven cavity and unsteady Kolmogorov flows.

Impact of compressiblity approximations on atmospheric gravity wave characteristics

Hrishikesh Sivanandan, Matthew Churchfield, Ben Kravitz, Eliot Quon, Mehtab Khan

Atmospheric gravity waves are a common phenomenon in the atmosphere and are of critical importance to understanding power generation from wind plants. Common sources of atmospheric gravity waves are stratified flow over topography or wind deceleration ahead of wind farms. Simulations to understand atmospheric boundary layer conditions and wind power generation often solve the Navier-Stokes equations in incompressible Boussinesq form, which allows for density variation as a function of potential temperature only; the purpose of this study is to understand how much that simplification matters. In this work, we evaluate simulated internal gravity wave characteristics by modeling stratified flow over bell-shaped hills using three forms of the governing equations: the linearized incompressible Boussinesq form, the full incompressible Boussinesq form, and the fully compressible form. While all formulations predict qualitatively similar wave structures, the compressible equations predict larger parcel displacements at higher altitudes, higher variations in apparent wavelengths, and more differences in wave angle, particularly at low Froude numbers. These differences can be attributed to compressibility and non-linear effects. The physical implications of differing wave characteristics become especially important in low Froude number regimes. These differences can, in turn, lead to differing predictions of wind farm performance and the characteristics of wind farm-induced gravity waves, underscoring the potential importance of compressibility effects in accurately capturing flow behavior in strongly stratified environments.

Elastic frontogenesis sets the spectra of smooth elastic turbulence

Marco Edoardo Rosti, Andrea Mazzino

Most turbulent flows are sustained by inertia, which transfers energy across scales and produces characteristic power-law spectra. Yet dilute polymer solutions also exhibit turbulence when inertia is negligible: in this state, known as elastic turbulence, the velocity field remains spatially smooth while both the velocity and the polymer conformation exhibit robust power-law spectra. How a smooth flow, lacking a hierarchy of eddies, generates such multiscale structure has remained unexplained. Here we show that the missing mechanism is elastic frontogenesis, whereby the flow spontaneously organizes polymer stress into thin sheets that are stretched within their plane and compressed across their thickness, causing their amplitude to grow as they become thinner. Because the fronts are sheet-like structures, this amplification-thinning law uniquely determines the polymer conformation spectrum, while the Stokes response transfers the same geometric scaling to the velocity field, reconciling broadband spectra with spatial smoothness. Being geometric rather than model-specific, elastic frontogenesis provides an organizing principle for viscoelastic flows and the viscoelastic analogue of vortex stretching in inertial turbulence.

Notably, by leveraging a highly optimized JAX backend, the network achieves convergence on a single consumer-grade GPU in less than four hours. Our approach offers a physics-consistent, real-time surrogate model that can substantially accelerate innovations in advanced microfluidic cooling of future electronics.

Beyond Fourier and Laplace: The Hankel Neural Operator for Learning Solution Operators of Differential Equations

Biswanath Barman, Rajendra K. Ray

Fourier and Laplace neural operators (FNO, LNO) learn kernel integral operators in a transform domain evaluated on a periodic grid. FNO has no explicit mechanism for boundaries, initial states or transients; LNO adds pole--residue symbols but evaluates them on the discrete Fourier grid with unconstrained poles. FNO's error is large on undamped oscillators and grows under grid refinement. We introduce the Hankel neural operator (HNO), derived from the structure of the solution operator. We prove that the solution operator of a well-posed linear constant-coefficient boundary-value problem on an interval is a Toeplitz operator with a rational kernel plus a finite-rank Hankel-type operator carrying boundary information. Classical results of Kronecker, Nehari and Adamyan--Arov--Krein characterise this kernel class: stable poles, finite Hankel rank and norm bounds independent of the interval length. HNO realises the class in three ways: a non-periodic convolution evaluated by the FFT, with a causal form; a closed-loop Lur'e operator for dynamics without fading memory; and a rational Fourier multiplier that contains FNO as a special case. Trained from data alone, HNO has the lowest mean test error on all nine LNO benchmarks and on the Burgers, Darcy and Navier--Stokes benchmarks of FNO; two of these differences, and the Navier--Stokes comparison with a parameter-matched FNO, are not statistically significant. On the six ODE problems it lowers the better baseline's mean error 2.1--5.2 times, and its Darcy error stays nearly constant from $85^2$ to $421^2$ grids, where FNO's grows eightfold. Ablations support each component. Code and data: \hnorepo{}.

Kinetic Non-Equilibrium Receptivity Analysis of Hypersonic Flows via a Complex Linearized Unified Gas-Kinetic Scheme

Yue Zhang, Caihong Su, Chao Li, Kun Xu

In the near-space regime, local rarefaction near the leading-edge shock and wall degrades Navier-Stokes-based stability and receptivity analyses due to velocity slip, temperature jumps, and non-equilibrium transport. The unified gas-kinetic scheme (UGKS) provides a multiscale kinetic description spanning continuum to rarefied regimes. Building on this, a complex linearized UGKS (CLUGKS) is developed: first-order perturbations of the discrete UGKS residual are formulated under a time-harmonic assumption with complex amplitudes, converting disturbance equations into a steady elliptic system solved via pseudo-time marching with LU-SGS acceleration. Consequently, kinetic shock structures, wall slip, temperature jumps, and non-Fourier heat fluxes are inherently captured without hybrid coupling. Three benchmarks verify the framework: a standing acoustic wave at an isothermal wall, a Mach 2 normal shock interacting with an obliquely incident acoustic wave, and freestream acoustic receptivity of a hypersonic blunt wedge. The scheme is applied to argon blunt-wedge flows at equivalent altitudes of 40 km and 50 km. At 40 km, UGKS and Navier-Stokes wall responses agree well in period and phase, while wall slip sustains a weakly positive spatial growth rate in UGKS downstream. At 50 km, intensified rarefaction produces a kinetic temperature precursor and a thickened, merged shock layer; while wall-pressure responses remain close, significant deviations emerge in temperature and heat-flux disturbances. These findings show that while continuum models capture dominant wall-pressure signatures under moderate rarefaction, resolving kinetic non-equilibrium across the shock-particularly its thermal footprint-is essential at higher altitudes.

We construct a lift-element-based (LE) network for analyzing extreme vortex gust-airfoil interactions. We consider three-dimensional flows around a NACA 0012 airfoil with a Taylor vortex gust at a Reynolds number of 1000 and compare the LE modes with those of a Biot--Savart (BS) network. Perturbations along the leading BS forcing modes amplify or attenuate the gust-induced lift variation, whereas those along the leading LE forcing modes yield a sustained lift increase or decrease. These input-output mode pairs, representing cause-and-effect relationships within the dynamic network, can guide the selection of actuation locations and timing for manipulating transient aerodynamic loads.
