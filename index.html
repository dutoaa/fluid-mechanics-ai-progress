Solving and Learning Navier-Stokes Dynamics with the Neural Basis Method

Yuhe Wang, Zhu Wang, Min Wang

Physics-informed machine learning has become a major direction in predictive modeling of fluid dynamics, combining governing physics with learned representations. Yet prevailing formulations neither guarantee that small PDE-residual losses imply small solution errors nor scale effectively with increasing problem size. Here we present the neural basis method (NBM) as a new approach for Navier--Stokes dynamics. NBM is built on a ``representation-then-projection'' principle: fixed neural bases define the representation space, while projection of the governing equations determines the expansion coefficients. The same bases define common coordinates for the parametric solution manifold, recasting operator learning as a parameter-to-coefficient mapping and enabling scalability by removing explicit spatial dependence. For incompressible flow, NBM yields a velocity-only formulation whose residual provides an a posteriori velocity-error estimate, establishing a certified, data-free training objective for coefficient learning. Theoretical analysis and numerical experiments demonstrate the accuracy, efficiency, generalization, and scalability of NBM, with substantial improvements over baselines.

Automated Discovery of Physically Interpretable Subgrid Length Scales for Detached Eddy Simulation via Agentic AI

Jiawei Chen, Xiang I. A. Yang, Ping He

Traditional turbulence-model development relies heavily on expert knowledge and is time-consuming. This study develops an agentic AI framework, DEScover (automated Discovery of DES closures), to explore subgrid length scales for detached eddy simulation (DES). The user provides only a modeling idea: the length scale is the grid width normal to the local vortex axis. Four large language model (LLM) agents then propose, review, implement, and evaluate candidate formulations and generate scripts for CFD simulations. The Discovery Agent infers the vortex axis from various vortex-identification methods. The candidates are tested on four cases spanning canonical and engineering flows: a flat plate, a backward-facing step, a free shear layer, and an iced airfoil. For all test cases, the best candidate consistently outperforms the standard length scale based on the maximum cell size. Its accuracy is similar to, or slightly better than, that of the expert-designed shear-layer-adapted length scale. Each agentic discovery run takes about 25 minutes on average, excluding the CFD simulations. These results show that LLM-based reasoning can significantly accelerate the discovery of physically interpretable turbulence models and reduce reliance on manual expert formulation.

When does boundary-condition uncertainty outweigh thermal and vegetation effects in street canyon flow? A global sensitivity analysis of input uncertainty

Albert Moreno Català, Joan Calafell Sandiumenge, Nikolaj T. Mücke, Jan Mateu Armengol

Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) is increasingly used to resolve wind, heat, and pollution patterns in cities with high fidelity but high computational cost. Thermal effects and vegetation are often neglected despite being important drivers, yet their influence may be masked by variability from other uncertain inputs. This work determines how precisely model parameters and boundary conditions must be known for these effects to govern output uncertainty in the velocity, temperature, and concentration fields of a two-dimensional street canyon. A global Sobol analysis of 24 parameters is made tractable by a surrogate trained on Reynolds-Averaged Navier--Stokes (RANS) simulations, combining principal component analysis (PCA) with multilayer perceptrons (MLPs). Two ventilation regimes assess the variance due to thermal and vegetation parameters as uncertainty in all other inputs is progressively reduced. At typical uncertainty levels, turbulence closure constants and inflow turbulence profiles govern variability in all three fields in both regimes. Under weak ventilation, solar forcing becomes the main source of temperature variability once the remaining uncertainties are halved, and of concentration variance once they are reduced to a quarter (about $\pm11\%$ for the dominant closure constant and $\pm12\%$ for the emission rate). In contrast, vegetation contributes significantly at typical uncertainty levels. Under strong ventilation, however, the canopy never overtakes the closure constants, even when the dominant one is known to $\pm5\%$. Spatially, both effects peak at road level and along canyon walls, which influence outdoor exposure and indoor infiltration, suggesting that high-resolution exposure studies should account for them.

Physics Informed Neural Network for Multi-dimensional Turbulent Self-gravitating Flows

Tirthankar De, Sayantan Auddy, Ramit Dey, Shantanu Basu

Molecular clouds exhibit complex, multi-scale dynamics driven by the interplay of turbulence, self-gravity, and gas pressure. Modelling these systems with conventional grid-based methods becomes increasingly expensive as spatial resolution and dimensionality increase. Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) offer a mesh-free alternative by incorporating the governing equations directly into the training objective, but their ability to model realistic turbulent self-gravitating flows remains largely unexplored. We apply PINNs to isothermal molecular clouds initialized with astrophysically motivated turbulent velocity fields, considering subsonic and supersonic evolution in two and three dimensions. We benchmark the predicted density and velocity fields against the finite-volume code Athena++. PINNs accurately recover the global evolution of the flow over multiple free-fall times across turbulent realizations, Mach numbers, and dimensionalities. Their training cost also exhibits weak dependence on spatial resolution, increasing by less than 25% from 128^3 to 1024^3 in our experiments. These results demonstrate that PINNs can model substantially more realistic self-gravitating turbulent flows than previously explored and provide a promising complementary framework for studying these systems.

Let's Do Turbulence Right: In Space and Time!

Maximilian Plattner, Fabian Paischer, Johannes Brandstetter

The three-dimensional Navier-Stokes equations govern fluid turbulence, a chaotic and non-linear physical phenomenon characterized by complex eddies interacting across vast spatial and temporal scales. A numerically accurate modeling of this multi-scale energy cascade requires fully resolving both the fine spatial structures and the continuous temporal evolution of the flow field. In this paper, we leverage massive, time-resolved direct numerical simulations, which exceed 28 TB per run, to assess how numerical choices govern the resolved (3+1)-dimensional physics of turbulent flows. Building on this analysis, we study how neural networks represent the recorded turbulent motion. Our results highlight that spatial and temporal assessments can disagree for both numerical records and learned representations. We therefore call on the machine learning community to treat (3+1)-dimensional turbulence as a four-dimensional phenomenon.

A Priori Estimation of the Non-Linear Compression Potential and Quantification of Autoencoder Non-Linearity in Turbulent Flows

Niklas Alexander Pissarski, Juan Pablo Murcia Leon, Søren Juhl Andersen

Data-driven reduced-order modelling (ROM) of turbulent flows is based on an efficient and physical low-dimensional representation of the flow process. Such a dimension reduction can be achieved using proper orthogonal decomposition (POD) or non-linear autoencoders. Capturing the inherent non-linearities in turbulent flows offers the potential for improved compression rates. This work proposes the local tangent residual (LTR) as an a priori estimate of the potential benefit of non-linear dimension reduction for a given dataset. The LTR is based on the local geometry of the data points, requiring neither prior model training nor the definition of a threshold parameter. For all datasets investigated in this work, namely, two vortex street cases and the turbulent wake flow of a wind turbine, the dominant trends in autoencoder compression performance are reproduced by the proposed LTR. The results are further contextualised through the decoder linearisation error (DLE). The DLE quantifies the non-linearity of the trained decoder by comparing it to a linearised surrogate model, which enables physical interpretability of the non-linearities. Together, a large non-linear compression potential in the turbulent flow, as quantified by the LTR, is corroborated by a large DLE, i.e., a pronounced non-linearity captured in the trained autoencoder. The LTR thus enables practitioners in turbulent flow modelling to determine a priori whether non-linear compression is worthwhile, avoiding the substantial computational cost of autoencoder architecture search and hyperparameter tuning in cases where POD already suffices.

Assisted Turbulence Modeling via Data Assimilation and Machine Learning for Turbomachinery Flows

Tom Moussie, Pierric Joseph, Marcello Meldi

This work presents the development and assessment of a hybrid framework coupling numerical simulation, experimental samples, data assimilation (DA) and machine learning (ML) to investigate the flow within an axial compressor stage. The methodology first implements a Multi-Fidelity Ensemble Kalman Filter (MFEnKF) which integrates experimental PIV data with a two-fidelity numerical simulation setup, combining steady-state RANS on a fixed grid with unsteady hybrid RANS/LES simulations on a moving grid. This data assimilation step successfully reduces discrepancies between experiments and CFD realizations. Besides, it provides additional high fidelity samples when compared with the available PIV data. This enriched database is used to train machine learning (ML) tools, with the objective to obtain black-box forcing terms to be included in the discretized form of the Navier-Stokes equations. These models are expected to preserve the accuracy of the DA results while not longer requiring the input of experimental data. The neural network architecture used here maps local physical features such as the velocity, the velocity gradients and the turbulent viscosity, to obtain the volume forcing terms. The analysis of the performance of such ML models shows that global accuracy is preserved but localized increased errors in specific regions of the compressor, such as close to the blade tips, can exceed those observed from the baseline RANS simulations. To resolve this issue, a Mixture of Experts (MoE) method is employed, which classifies and combines the trained ML tools for different flow regions. The discrepancy with experimental data of this method is systematically lower than the one observed for the baseline RANS solution and very similar to the one obtained with the MFEnKF.

Takeru Hasegawa, Naoto Mitsume, Shota Deguchi, Mitsuteru Asai, Tetsuya Kanagawa

Interactions between ultrasound and microbubbles underlie a range of biomedical and industrial applications, yet predicting how the physical parameters of the medium and bubbles govern nonlinear wave propagation remains difficult. This study proposes a parameter estimation framework that combines a Korteweg-de Vries-Burgers (KdVB) equation, describing weakly nonlinear wave propagation in a bubbly liquid, with a physics-informed neural network (PINN). The contributions are threefold. First, the PINN reproduces the reference solution of the governing equation in the forward problem, with a near-zero prediction error at the initial time that confirms the initial condition is enforced exactly as intended. Second, prior to inverse estimation, a sensitivity analysis is introduced that quantifies, at both the coefficient and solution levels, how each of eight physical parameters influences the KdVB equation, allowing the expected difficulty of estimating each parameter to be assessed in advance. Third, based on this assessment, single-parameter inverse estimation is performed for seven of the eight parameters using the PINN. Liquid viscosity, bubble initial radius, liquid density, sound speed, and liquid pressure are estimated with errors of 0.8-2.1%, the polytropic exponent converges only partially, and the surface tension coefficient cannot be estimated at all, consistent with the sensitivity analysis. These results establish a general framework for identifying and estimating the physical parameters governing nonlinear wave propagation in bubbly liquids from observed waveform data.

Physics-informed neural network (PINN) approximations of capillary menisci in polygonal channels

Zijian Liu, Julian Olszewski, Jie Xu

In a zero-gravity cylindrical container, a meniscus can span a square or equilateral-triangular cross-section only at contact angles at or above the respective Concus-Finn critical angles of 45 degrees and 60 degrees. We study strong-form physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) across these thresholds under fixed collocation and optimization budgets. Classical capillary flux balance yields a lower bound on the combined interior and wall residual loss for arbitrary positive sampling densities. In the tested nonexistence regime, several degrees below the critical angles, independently evaluated network losses are 1.6-1.9 times this bound. A polynomial least-squares comparison supports the same geometric interpretation. The PINNs still return smooth graphs with finite axial extent, even though the prescribed equilibrium problem has no spanning graph solution. Their small interior residuals coexist with wall-flux deficits near corners, where adjoining contact-angle conditions demand more flux than a finite-slope graph can supply. Residuals vary on both sides of the critical angle, while the bound tends uniformly to zero at threshold for fixed wall weight. These results give a geometric scale for interpreting the computed loss and locate the incompatibility that a small interior residual can conceal.

DiffVortex: A quantum-inspired fast solver for incompressible flows

Yu Jun Loo, Silas Alben, Shravan Veerapaneni

Matrix-product-state (MPS) and quantized tensor-train (QTT) representations offer a promising framework for computational fluid dynamics, with logarithmic run time and memory scaling in mesh size when bond dimensions remain bounded. Existing MPS-based solvers are Eulerian: they compress velocity fields and enforce immersed-body boundary conditions through static masks or body-fitted coordinates. Moving or flexible bodies complicate this approach: deformation requires updating MPS masks every time step, while filamentary vorticity in high-Reynolds-number flows may resist low-rank compression. We propose a complementary approach: compress the Biot--Savart kernel rather than the flow state, evolve vorticity using Lagrangian vortex particles, and represent immersed bodies by bound vortex sheets on Lagrangian surface points. Our main theoretical result is a lifting theorem: QTT rank $r$ of the kernel implies matrix-product-operator (MPO) rank at most $2^d r$ for the corresponding convolution operator in $d$ dimensions, independent of grid resolution. This bound is sharp for our interleaved ordering. With quantics tensor cross interpolation, the moment-to-local stage of a single-level fast multipole method becomes a precomputed tensor-network contraction with quasi-linear complexity, retaining Lagrangian flexibility for immersed bodies. Our PyTorch implementation (\texttt{DiffVortex}) is end-to-end differentiable, runs on GPUs, and handles $6.7\times10^{7}$ particles on a single device. We validate against reference vortex-method results for an impulsively started cylinder and exact and reference solutions for inviscid elliptical vortices. We also demonstrate two-dimensional flows past rigidly moving, multiple, and deforming bodies, including gradients propagated through the full time integration.
